# Adaptive Design Scaling

Use when a concept is larger than the stated team, timeline, engine, content budget, or platform can support. Adaptive design scaling preserves the core player fantasy while producing solo, indie, AA, and AAA variants with explicit production cuts, risk tradeoffs, and validation gates.

## What To Produce

- Fantasy lock: the one player promise that cannot be cut, such as "command a haunted train crew," "survive a faction war," or "master expressive grappling combat."
- Scale ladder: solo, indie, AA, and AAA versions with scope, team size, timeline, content count, engine assumptions, and launch model.
- System triage: must-keep mechanics, scalable mechanics, deferred mechanics, and removed mechanics.
- Content budget: levels, biomes, enemies, bosses, companions, weapons, quests, cinematics, live-ops beats, and procedural breadth per scale.
- Production constraints: animation load, art-kit reuse, networking complexity, QA matrix, localization, platform certification, save migration, telemetry, and patch burden.
- Preservation proof: explain how every smaller variant still fulfills the fantasy without pretending to be the larger version.

## Scaling Method

1. Name the fantasy, emotional goal, genre promise, target platform, team size, and timebox.
2. Identify the expensive drivers: networking, open world, cinematics, VO, bespoke animation, live ops, procedural breadth, AI complexity, asset count, and QA.
3. Build the smallest version first. A solo version should be playable, readable, and complete, not a pitch for a bigger game.
4. Add scale in layers: more content, more systemic depth, more production polish, more live operations, and more platform breadth.
5. Mark cuts as creative choices. A cut that preserves fantasy is good design, not failure.
6. Add validation gates for each scale: vertical-slice proof, content pipeline proof, playtest threshold, performance budget, and production stop/go criteria.

## Variant Ladder

| Scale | Team Shape | Best Use | Cut Pressure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solo | 1 creator, store assets, small codebase | Core fantasy proof, festival demo, portfolio game | Networking, VO, huge worlds, bespoke animation |
| Indie | 2-10 people, reusable kits, focused pipeline | Commercial premium or small live game | Fewer biomes, fewer roles, limited cinematics |
| AA | 10-60 people, discipline leads, outsourced support | Multi-system commercial title | Production coordination, QA, content dependency risk |
| AAA | 60+ people, dedicated departments, long roadmap | Cinematic, open-world, live-service, or franchise play | Cost, schedule, governance, live support burden |

## Production Rules

- Preserve the fantasy through verbs, not content volume. If the fantasy is "lead a rebellion," a solo game can use one district and three characters.
- Never scale by only adding more enemies, quests, or currencies. Add meaningful systemic depth or presentation only when the pipeline supports it.
- Give every variant a release shape: prototype, vertical slice, Steam demo, premium launch, seasonal launch, expansion, or franchise roadmap.
- Name non-negotiable accessibility and performance commitments at every scale.
- Keep monetization aligned with scale. A small premium game should not inherit live-service economy overhead.

## Anti-Patterns

- Calling an MMO idea "indie" without reducing networking, moderation, content cadence, and live-ops cost.
- Removing the mechanic that carries the fantasy while preserving expensive decoration.
- Producing only the AAA dream and no feasible smaller path.
- Treating procedural generation as a replacement for authored quality, validation, or QA.
- Scaling visuals up while leaving onboarding, accessibility, telemetry, and production staffing undefined.

## Agent Checklist

- What is the player fantasy that must survive every scale?
- What is the smallest complete version that can ship?
- Which systems are kept, simplified, deferred, or removed at each scale?
- Which content counts, team roles, timeline assumptions, and QA risks change by scale?
- What stop/go gate proves each variant is feasible before production expands?
